02 Why they need it — the audit

What we found on rockraymondproductionsinc.com on Nov 17, 2026. Every item here is a line in the pitch — each one costs them members.

High

The whole business lives on one endless page

Radio, memberships, album promos, awards, the roster, the company bio, and a full press release are all stacked on a single scrolling homepage. There are no real pages for Artists, Radio, or About — so Google can't rank them separately, and a visitor hunting for the membership has to scroll past album promos to find it. The product is buried in their own homepage.

High

Top links that go nowhere

Header/navigation items point at placeholder and unfinished routes (the site still carries default Squarespace-style paths like /new-page and /new-page-1), and key images link out to unrelated places — the main banner image links to Kylie Willow's separate site, and album art links off to Qobuz. A prospect who clicks the wrong hero image leaves the site before ever seeing a price.

High

The footer names the wrong domain

The copyright line at the bottom reads “© Copyright rockraymondproductions.com” — a different domain from the actual site, rockraymondproductionsinc.com. Anyone who types what the footer says lands somewhere else. It's a small typo that quietly leaks their own traffic.

Med

Memberships are sold like blog posts

The four plans ($20 / $40 / $60 monthly, $170 annual) sit mid-page as store “Quick View” tiles with long paragraph descriptions. There's no comparison table, no “most popular” anchor, and no single checkout path — yet this is a recurring-revenue subscription business presented like a merch shelf.

Med

Submissions run through a Gmail inbox

Artists are told to email MP3s to a Gmail address to get on the playlist. With “hundreds of new tracks arriving every week” (their words), that means no tracking, no status, no follow-up sequence — and no contact database being built from the most valuable list they have: working AI musicians.

Med

Duplicate and unlabeled imagery throughout

The same placeholder artwork repeats across sections, roster “spotlights” are giant stacked images with no names or links attached, and the press quote block runs straight into a raw URL. It reads as a work-in-progress — for a company asking artists to pay monthly for professional promotion.

Low

Good bones, hidden

The raw material is genuinely strong: a real “world's first” claim, an EIN Presswire launch (May 31, 2026), a flagship artist with five albums, a countdown show, and a sister PR brand (Hype Mechanics PR). None of it is structured so a first-time visitor — or a search engine — can find it in ten seconds.
